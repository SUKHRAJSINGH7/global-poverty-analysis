## The World Got Less Poor — But Nobody Got More Equal
In 2020, governments worldwide celebrated falling poverty numbers.
But the data tells a different story.
While global poverty dropped from 25% to 21% between 2015 and 2024,
inequality barely moved. 21 countries got less poor AND more unequal
at the same time. The gap between rich and poor didn't shrink —
it just got quieter.

## Before COVID — A Decade of Progress
From 2015 to 2019 the world was on track. Global average poverty
fell steadily from 25.25% to 23.85%. Sub-Saharan Africa was still
stuck above 38% but the trend was clear — things were improving.
Then 2020 happened.

## The COVID Shock
Strangely, global poverty numbers dropped in 2020 to 21.33%.
Governments pumped trillions into stimulus programs — cash transfers,
unemployment benefits, food assistance. The numbers looked good on paper.
Then the stimulus ended.
In 2021 poverty rebounded to 22.73%. The real COVID effect had arrived —
delayed, but devastating for the most vulnerable nations.
Mali was hit hardest. Poverty spiked +11.37 points in two years —
from 61% to 72.5%. Myanmar (+3.98) and Afghanistan (+1.57) followed.
Even France and Germany felt it, though at a fraction of the scale.
The scale difference is staggering — poor nations were hit 20x harder
than wealthy ones.

## Who Suffered Most
Not all regions are equal. Sub-Saharan Africa averaged 37.84% poverty
across the decade — 36 times higher than North America's 1.05%.
Latin America told the most surprising story — nearly halving its
poverty rate from 18% to 10% over 10 years, the strongest improvement
of any region in the dataset.
Meanwhile the Middle East & North Africa got worse after 2021.
By 2024 their poverty rate was higher than during COVID. No recovery.
Just continued decline.
Southeast Asia showed a clear COVID spike — poverty jumped from 16%
to 18.45% in 2021 — but recovered strongly by 2024.

## The Hidden Story — Inequality
Here's what the headlines missed.
Even as poverty rates improved, inequality stagnated. The global Gini
coefficient — which measures the gap between rich and poor within
countries — barely moved from 42.27 to 42.22 over six years.
21 countries reduced poverty AND increased inequality simultaneously.
Morocco's Gini jumped 5.3 points while poverty barely changed.
South Africa and Colombia improved poverty numbers but their Gini
pushed above 48 — among the highest in the world.
Even wealthy nations weren't immune. France and the UK both appeared
in the list of countries with rising inequality despite falling poverty.
The poor got slightly less poor. The rich got much richer.

## Where Are We Now
By 2024, 36 of 40 countries had recovered to below their 2019
poverty levels. That's genuinely good news.
But four countries didn't make it back:

Yemen: 51.5% → 55.94% — still getting worse in 2024
Mali: 46.27% → 49.47% — COVID damage not reversed
Egypt: 22.38% → 23.16% — slow deterioration
Nepal: 53.87% → 54.05% — marginal but still moving backwards

The GDP data tells a brutal but predictable story. The USA with
$84,602 GDP per capita has 0.53% poverty. Afghanistan with $563
GDP per capita has 48.79% poverty. The correlation between economic
output and poverty is almost perfect — and almost impossible to escape.

So What
Poverty metrics alone don't tell the full story. A country can
"recover" on paper while its middle class shrinks and its billionaires
multiply. Policymakers, NGOs, and international bodies need to track
both poverty AND inequality — because you can win one battle and lose
the war.
The data is clear:

The world is getting less poor ✅
The world is not getting more equal ❌
COVID's real damage was delayed and unequal ❌
Four countries are still going backwards in 2024 ❌

The headline number looks good. The full picture is more complicated.

## Methodology
Data: Global Poverty & Economic Inequality 2015–2024 via Kaggle
Countries: 40 countries across 10 regions
Period: 2015–2024 (10 years)
Tools: SQL (DuckDB) for analysis · Tableau Public for visualization
Limitations: Self-reported national data · Reporting lags may
affect 2020–2021 accuracy · Dataset covers 40 of ~195 countries
so findings may not represent all nations equally
